Step 1 of 4 · Your property

Find the property, then check its line on the photo. The shade is worked out over the whole lot and nothing more, so the line has to be right: the county's record is used when there is one, and you trace the corners yourself when there is not.

Step 2 of 4 · The trees

USGS lidar over the lot, read in your browser and lined up with the photo, becomes the trees and buildings the shade map uses: every block is drawn from the lowest to the highest height where the laser found something that stops light, tilted so you can look under the crowns. If a tree is gone, or you would rather see the lawn without one, erase it here and the shade is worked out without it.

Step 3 of 4 · The shade map

The sun is traced over the trees for any day of the year, with the leaves as the weather here puts them. Colors over the lawn show hours of direct sun, the day's light, the shadows at one time, or the light averaged over the growing season. Tap a spot to read it, or draw a box to average an area.

Step 4 of 4 · Which grass

The climate here says cool-season or warm-season grass; the light through the growing season, point by point over the lawn, says which of those will hold the shade, and how much of the lawn each would hold. Then when to plant it.
