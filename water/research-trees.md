## HARD FINDINGS (measured, sourced)

### 1. How far roots spread

- **Gilman 1988, *J. Arboriculture* 14(4):85-87**, "Predicting root spread from trunk diameter and branch spread" [read: abstract page]. These were young trees, planted as nursery stock in NJ and FL, the NJ ones dug about three years after planting. Root spread grew linearly with crown spread in five species. The mean maximum spread was **2.9x the dripline radius**, ranging from **1.68x (green ash) to 3.77x (southern magnolia)**. Live oak showed no relation. The roots farthest from the trunk were near the surface. The authors warn the relation is unproven for older trees.
- **Gilman 1988, *HortScience* 23(2):351-353** [2nd: abstract]. **77% (poplar), 59% (honeylocust) and 54% (green ash)** of the root system lay beyond the dripline.
- **Schnelle, Feucht & Klett 1989, *J. Arboric.* 15(9):201-205** [read: abstract page]. A review that repeats Gilman's numbers. It also reports sugar maple roots about 30 ft beyond the branch tips, and Colorado blue spruce with about 60% of absorbing roots beyond the dripline. Gilman 1988 cites that same Watson spruce result differently ("60% … in the drip-irrigated zone"), so treat the spruce figure as unsettled.
- **Perry 1982, *J. Arboric.* 8(8):197-211** [read: abstract]. About 99% of roots are in the top metre. Roots spread over an area **1-2+ times the tree's height** across. This is a review statement, not one dataset.
- **Whitcomb 1987, Proc. Florida Turf-Grass Mgmt Conf.** [read]. "The area influenced by an established tree extends at least **1½ times** the distance from the base of the tree to the outer most branches." This is a recommendation drawn from pot experiments, not a field measurement.
- Stone & Kalisz 1991 (*Forest Ecol. Manage.* 46:59-102) is the standard review of maximum root extent [not read: I could not retrieve it and quote no numbers from it].

**By species group.** The only per-species ratios are Gilman's, on young trees. Ash is the narrowest (1.7x), magnolia the widest (3.8x), and the mean is about 2.9x. I found **no measured ratio for mature maples, oaks or spruces in lawns**.

### 2. How deep the roots are, and how much is in the turf layer

- **Jackson et al. 1996, *Oecologia* 108:389-411** [read, full text]. The cumulative root fraction follows Y = 1 − β^d, with d in cm. β values: **temperate deciduous forest 0.966, temperate coniferous 0.976, temperate grassland 0.943**. For all trees, β = 0.970, which means **26% of root biomass in the top 10 cm, 60% in the top 30 cm and 78% in the top 50 cm**. Grasses have 44% in the top 10 cm and 75% in the top 30 cm. DERIVED for the tool's 20 cm turf root zone: deciduous **50%**, coniferous **38%**, all trees 46%. These are total root *biomass* in forests and grasslands, not uptake, and not lawn trees.
- **Yanai et al. 2008, *Can. J. For. Res.* 38** [read]. Northern hardwoods in New Hampshire, sampled only to about 25 cm. Within that depth, the median fine root (<2 mm) sat at **12.0 cm for sugar maple, 10.0 cm for beech and 9.7 cm for yellow birch**. Fine roots are shallower than coarse roots.
- **Watson 1988, *J. Arboric.* 14(8):200-203** [read: abstract page]. Seven species, about 20 years old, including green ash, linden, pin and red oak, and sugar, red and Norway maple. Compared with grass, fine root surface area rose **up to 113% when the grass was removed and up to 195% under mulch**. The exception was the oaks, whose roots barely responded. Sugar maple rose 113% with the grass gone. The **7.5-15 cm layer under turf was the driest** of all treatments. **Turf suppresses tree fine roots in the top layer**, so a lawn tree probably has *less* of its uptake in the top 20 cm than a forest tree.
- **Whitcomb 1987** [read]. The pots were arranged so every treatment got the same shade. Shallow-rooted **silver maple cut Kentucky bluegrass root mass by up to 59%**, and deep-rooted **honeylocust by 35%**. Sod yield fell much less. Established turf was unaffected in the first season, and the tree "wins" over years as grass roots turn over. In the reverse case, turf cut silver maple roots and left honeylocust roots alone, because those sat deeper. **Whitcomb & Roberts 1973, *Agron. J.* 65:126-129** [2nd] reports silver maple roots disappearing from the top centimetre once bluegrass was seeded.
- **Messenger 1976, *J. Arboric.* 2(12):228-230** [read]. A review of grass-on-tree interference: nutrient competition (foliar N), and turf roots limiting tree root depth and spread. It gives no water numbers.
- **Dawson 1993, *Oecologia* 95:565-574** [2nd]. Mature sugar maples **hydraulically lift** deep water into shallow soil at night. Neighbours within about 3 m used it and grew better. So a maple can *add* water to the turf layer as well as remove it.

### 3. How much water a tree uses

- **Peters, McFadden & Montgomery 2010, *J. Geophys. Res.* 115, G04006** [read: abstract]. Sap flow in a **Minneapolis-St Paul suburb**, the closest climate analogue I found. Growing-season mean transpiration per m² of projected crown was **1.11 mm/d for deciduous broadleaf trees and 1.90 mm/d for evergreen conifers**. Annual totals were 153 and 307 mm. The conifers had a smaller crown area (31 vs 74 m²), a higher LAI (8.8 vs 5.5) and an 8-month rather than 4-month season. Ring-porous trees (oak, ash) and diffuse-porous trees (maple) regulated stomata differently. DERIVED: assuming a growing-season ET0 of about 4 mm/d, which I have not sourced for MSP, deciduous trees used **about 0.3x ET0 per crown m²**.
- **Wullschleger, Meinzer & Vertessy 1998, *Tree Physiol.* 18:499-512** [read: abstract]. Across 52 studies and 67 species, almost **90% of maximum daily water use fell between 10 and 200 kg/tree/day**, for trees averaging 21 m tall.
- **Pittenger & Shaw 2013, Proc. Irrigation Show, IA** [read, full text]. These are UC extension horticulturists. Trees, shrubs and groundcovers need about **0.5x ET0** in dry-summer climates. **A plant factor of 0.7 is more appropriate in humid climates with summer rain**, which covers Michigan. For isolated trees or canopy cover under 75%, water need is **ET0 × PF × crown projection area**. They say crown area, not land area, should be used.
- **Lindsey & Bassuk 1991, *J. Arboric.* 17(6):141-149** [read: article page]. Their design formula for Ithaca, NY is **daily use = crown projection × LAI (4) × pan evaporation × 0.20**. DERIVED: taking pan at about 1.25x ET0, this is about **1.0x ET0 per crown m²**. It is a conservative sizing rule, not a measurement.
- **Nielsen, Bühler & Kristoffersen 2007, *Arboric. Urban For.* 33(4):231** [read: article page]. TDR measurements put water loss at about **10 L/day** for young park lindens in Copenhagen, 7-8 years old. They cite about 150 L/day for 23 m beech.
- **WUCOLS** (Costello & Jones) [2nd]. Bands are high 0.7-0.9, moderate 0.4-0.6, low 0.1-0.3 and very low <0.1. These are California regional ratings with no Michigan region. I could not open the 2000 guide itself.

**Spread of estimates per crown m², as a multiple of ET0:** about 0.3 (Minnesota sap flow), 0.5-0.7 (extension plant factors) and about 1.0 (Lindsey & Bassuk sizing). That is roughly a **threefold uncertainty**. Pittenger & Shaw, and the sources they cite, also note that isolated trees respond **non-linearly** to ET0 and cut transpiration at high evaporative demand. Linear scaling therefore overstates use on the hottest days.

### 4. Trees competing with turf

- **Shade vs roots, separated by experiment (crops, not turf).** Jose, Gillespie et al. 2000, *Agroforestry Systems* 48 ("Defining competition vectors…", parts 1-2) [2nd: abstracts]. Black walnut and red oak with maize, Indiana. Polyethylene root barriers or trenches removed root competition and raised maize leaf area and yield. They concluded "**competition for water rather than … light**" set productivity. This is the clearest temperate Midwest evidence that roots can matter more than shade, but it concerns maize, not turf.
- **Turf:** golf-course agronomy articles (USGA Green Section, 1973 *Golf Course* piece) [2nd] report root-pruning trenches 24-30 in deep restoring turf near trees that cast little shade. These are anecdotes, not measurements.
- **Watson 1988** (above): turf, not trees, dried the 7.5-15 cm layer the most.
- **Pittenger & Shaw 2013** [read]: when groundcover grows under a tree canopy, "the water use of the understory is negligible." Layering does not raise total demand.
- **I found no study that measured soil moisture transects outward from trunks in a lawn.** The urban soil-drying studies I found (Melbourne eucalypts, *Plant Soil* 2023; desiccation to about 0.6-0.7x tree height laterally) [2nd] are on deep shrink-swell clays and not comparable.
- **Bijoor et al. 2012, *Urban Ecosystems*** [2nd]: isotopes show Los Angeles urban trees transpire a mix of irrigation water and groundwater. Trees in watered lawns do draw on the lawn's water.

### 5. How other models handle trees

- **Landscape Coefficient Method / WUCOLS** (Costello, Matheny & Clark 1993/2000) [2nd, plus Pittenger & Shaw read]. K_L = species × density × microclimate, with density 0.5-1.3. Pittenger & Shaw argue a density factor above 1 for trees over turf "is not defensible." It is a lumped model per hydrozone with no spatial apportioning.
- **i-Tree Hydro** (Wang, Endreny & Nowak 2008, *JAWRA* 44:75-85) [2nd]. Semi-distributed and hourly. It tracks tree cover over pervious and impervious ground as cover fractions, uses LAI-based interception, and computes ET by modified Penman-Monteith. It is lumped by cover fraction, with no root geometry.
- **SWAT** (Neitsch et al., Theoretical Documentation) [2nd]. Uptake is exponential with depth, about 50% from the top 6% of the root depth (a reviewer's summary). It is 1-D only.
- **SWMM LID controls**: as far as I know these carry no tree-root uptake term [not verified].
- **Vrugt, van Wijk, Hopmans & Šimůnek 2001, *Water Resour. Res.* 37:2457-2470** [2nd]. It gives 1-, 2- and 3-D uptake distributions fitted to soil-water data around a sprinkler-irrigated almond. It is used in HYDRUS. The 2-D radial form, from memory and not re-read, is β(r,z) = (1 − r/R_m)(1 − z/Z_m)·exp(−(p_r/R_m)|r* − r| − (p_z/Z_m)|z* − z|). It is a cone with an exponential bias, and is suitable for a grid. Check the paper before using its parameters.

## 6. Uncertainties, largest first

1. **Tree use per crown m² relative to ET0** (0.3-1.0, threefold), and its non-linear response at high ET0.
2. **Share of uptake from the top 20 cm.** Forest root biomass gives 38-50%. Turf suppresses tree surface roots (Watson 1988), which points lower. Trees in watered lawns may favour the wet top layer (Bijoor 2012), which points higher. Hydraulic lift may hand water back (Dawson 1993). **No lawn measurement found.**
3. **Lateral extent** (1.5-3.8x crown radius), measured only on young trees.
4. Species: ash is narrow, honeylocust deep, silver maple shallow and aggressive, oaks barely responsive to turf removal. Lidar does not give species, though it can tell conifer from broadleaf (crown shape, leaf-off returns).
5. Roots under pavement and foundations, and toward the watered lawn, are unknown.

**A cheap field check:**
- Equipment: a handheld soil-moisture probe, a TDR or capacitance meter with 12 cm and 20 cm rods. Use one maple and one spruce on lawns the tool already maps.
- Points: 0.5R, 1R, 1.5R, 2R, 3R and an open-lawn control, along **north and south** bearings. Shade is asymmetric and roots roughly symmetric, so south-side points beyond the dripline isolate root draw with little shade, while north-side points show shade plus roots.
- Timing: read the day after a soaking rain, then every 2-3 dry days for a week. The slope of each drying curve is that cell's daily loss, which can be compared directly with the balance.
- A free second check: NAIP greenness (already in `water/lib/naip.js`) in a dry summer, as a ring profile around isolated trees.

## SPECULATION: a proposed grid treatment (model choices, not established)

**Which trees.** Each lidar crown polygon gives its area A (m²), an equivalent radius R = √(A/π) and a trunk point at the crown peak. Split crowns into conifer and broadleaf if the lidar allows it; otherwise treat all as broadleaf.

**Daily use per tree** (litres = mm × m²):

  T = K_t × ET0 × A × S

- K_t: broadleaf **0.4** (range 0.25-0.8), conifer **0.5** (0.3-0.9).
  - The default sits between Peters 2010 (about 0.3) and Pittenger's humid plant factor (0.7).
  - It would be a slider in FINDINGS until the transect is measured.
- S is the season:
  - broadleaf leaf-on, mid-May to mid-October (dates to be confirmed for Michigan);
  - conifers whenever the soil is unfrozen and ET0 > 0.
- Optional cap for the non-linear response: T ≤ K_t × A × min(ET0, 5 mm). The 5 mm is a guess.

**Share from the turf root zone:** f = **0.4** (0.25-0.55). This starts from Jackson's 38-50% in the top 20 cm and is nudged down for turf suppression (Watson 1988). The rest goes to deeper soil the balance does not model, and is ignored.

**Spatial weights.** Spread the tree's draw over a disc of radius R_root = k × R, with k = **2** (1.5-3; Whitcomb 1.5, Gilman mean 2.9). Use a cone, w(d) = 1 − d/R_root, which is Vrugt's radial term without the exponential factor.
- Normalise the weights over all **non-building** cells in the disc, since roots run under paving.
- Withdraw only from cells the balance carries (lawn and beds). The share that falls under pavement leaves the turf water alone.
- Daily draw from cell j: U_j = f × T × w_j / Σw, summed over overlapping trees.

**Stress.** Multiply U_j by the same FAO Ks the turf uses in that cell. The tree then backs off as the cell dries, rather than pulling it below wilting. This also roughly stands in for the shift of uptake to deeper water.

**Shade stays as it is** (Danielson & Feldhake). The two effects add: shade lowers the turf's ET, and roots add U_j.

**What the defaults imply.** Worked example, DERIVED, to show the scale:
- A maple with R = 6 m (A = 113 m²) on a 5 mm ET0 day: T = 0.4 × 5 × 113 = 226 L/d. That sits inside the 10-200 kg/d sap-flow range and above Peters' mean of about 125 L/d.
- Draw from the turf layer: f × T = 90 L, over a 12 m disc of 452 m².
- That is a mean of **0.2 mm/d** and a peak of about **0.6 mm/d** near the trunk.
- Open turf uses 0.8 × 5 = **4 mm/d**, so the tree adds about 5% on average and about 15% near the trunk.
- Under the crown, shade at light 0.4 already removes about 1.7 mm/d of turf ET.

So under these defaults, **trees barely change the turf's water budget, cell by cell**: near-trunk cells stay wetter than open lawn (shade outweighs the roots), and the effect beyond the dripline is small. Only at the top of all three ranges (K_t 0.8, f 0.55, k 1.5) does the draw become large: a mean of about 1 mm/d over the disc and about 3 mm/d near the trunk, more than the shade saving. So the answer depends almost entirely on where the true values sit in those ranges.

If the field transect shows much faster drying near trees than this, the likely missing pieces are:
- a shallower effective turf root zone under shade;
- rain interception by the crown, which I have not researched;
- a higher f in watered lawns.

**Biggest unknowns to settle before building:** K_t (threefold), f (no lawn data) and k. The transect above measures their combined effect directly: the drying slope against distance.

**Candidate FINDINGS line:** trees enter as shade (sourced) plus a root draw of K_t × ET0 × crown area × f, spread as a cone to k × R. All three parameters are SPECULATION pending a probe transect.
