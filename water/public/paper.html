Summary

The water model splits a lawn into watering zones. For each zone it says how much to water and how often, month by month, and when to water next this week. It works on a grid of 1 m squares (a little over 3 ft) and keeps a daily account of the water in every square over ten past years of weather, 2016 to 2025.

Nearly every piece of it is a published method in wide use. Grass water use and the soil water account follow the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization's FAO-56 method [1]. Runoff follows the USDA curve number method [2]. Soil comes from the USDA soil survey [3]. Water rising from a water table follows FAO's AquaCrop model [4]. The new part is the combination: these methods run together at the scale of one yard, from free public data, with each square's own sun, shade, slope, wind and soil.

Several pieces have been checked against outside measurements, with results in section 11. The sunlight data agreed with research radiometers to within 1%. Roofs and paving found from lidar agreed with a 1 m land cover map on about 85% of squares. The water-table rule was fitted to 227 shallow wells. The developer's own lawn needs about an inch a week in July over 2 to 3 waterings, and the model gives a close figure.

The central output has not yet been checked against soil moisture measured on a lawn: which parts dry out first, and how fast. Field readings with a soil moisture meter began in November 2026. Until those results are in, the zones and amounts are the model's estimate, built from measured inputs and published methods, not a measured result. The largest known open question is the weather-based water use figure, which runs 15 to 20% above one Michigan weather network's figures (section 5).

1. What it gives you

  • Zones. Up to four, grouped by how often each part of the lawn needs water in its thirstiest month. Sunny slopes, shaded corners, wet hollows and the strip below a downspout tend to land in different zones.
  • How much and how often, by month. For each zone, the number of waterings in a typical month and the depth each time in inches, with the range seen across the ten years.
  • This week. The next watering date and amount, from the last 60 days of weather and the National Weather Service forecast.
  • Run times. With sprinklers mapped, minutes per valve, split into soak cycles where the ground takes water slowly.
  • Maps. Where water collects, the water table depth, slope, shade, wind shelter, the soil, and heat: what warms or cools each part of the lawn, with an estimate of its surface temperature.

The person using it supplies a traced outline of the lawn, the property line, where the downspouts empty, the grass species, whether topsoil was brought in, and the watering level they want (normal, deficit or dormancy).

3. The daily water account

Each square of lawn is treated as a bucket: the soil the roots can reach. Water comes in as rain, as water running in from uphill, as roof water from a downspout, as sprinkler water, and as water rising from below. It leaves as the grass's use, as runoff downhill, and as drainage below the roots.

The size of the bucket is the water the roots can use between "full" (field capacity) and "too dry to give up any more" (the wilting point). Both depend on the soil. By volume, a sand is full at about 6% water and at its wilting point by about 2%; a sandy loam runs from about 19% down to 9%; a loam from about 23% to 12%; a clay loam from about 31% to 19% (class averages computed in the EPA's HELP model documentation [32] from Rawls and others' measured soil-class means [33]). So a foot of sandy loam holds about 1.3 in the grass can use, and a foot of sand under half an inch. The model adds this up layer by layer from the soil survey's own figures for each layer, down to that day's root depth, so a sandy lawn gets a small bucket and a loam a large one.

Grass does not wait until the wilting point to suffer. It starts to slow down once a set share of the usable water is gone: 40% of it for cool-season grasses and 50% for warm-season grasses, from FAO-56's table for turf [1]. Those are shares of the bucket, not soil moisture readings. On a sandy loam with roots 6 in deep, 40% is about 0.25 in of water, and the soil would read about 15% by volume at that point; on a loam it is about 0.3 in. The ideal watering refills a square on the morning it would cross that line.

That share moves with the weather and the soil, as FAO-56 sets out, and the model follows it. When the grass is drawing water fast, the drying soil cannot pass water to the roots quickly enough, so stress comes sooner, with more water still in the soil. When it is drawing slowly, the grass can use more of the bucket first. FAO-56 sets the 40% at a use of 0.2 in (5 mm) a day and moves it 4 points for each millimetre a day above or below that. The model uses each square's own use over the past week, so shade and shelter count. Sands get a line 7.5% higher (they give up their water easily until it is nearly gone) and clays 7.5% lower (they hold the last of it tightly).

In practice, lawns in a climate like Michigan's seldom use 0.2 in a day, even in July and least of all in shade, so the line mostly sits above 40%. On the developer's lawn the change cut the year's watering by about 16% (7.1 to 6.0 in), mostly in spring, fall and the shady zones. The sunniest zone's July watering did not change (10 waterings).

Water beyond "full" can sit briefly in the soil before it drains. When the soil can hold no more, extra water runs off to the next square downhill. Low spots hold their share until it soaks in or dries.

Technical note FAO-56 single-coefficient root-zone balance, run daily per 1 m cell [1]: depletion Dr rises with ETc and falls with infiltration and capillary rise. TAW = Σ θawc,j·Δzj over the day's root depth, from SSURGO awc_r. RAW = p·TAW with p fixed at 0.40 (cool) or 0.50 (warm); FAO-56's adjustments of p are applied: each day p = psoil + 0.04(5 − ETc), held to 0.1–0.8, with ETc the cell's past-week mean (ET0 × Kc × the cell's shade, wind and heat factor) and psoil = ptable × 1.075 for sand and loamy sand, × 0.925 for clay, silty clay and sandy clay (FAO-56's 5–10%, taken at its middle), by the texture of the root zone's top layer. The zones' depths use p at 5 mm/day. Ks = (TAW − Dr)/(TAW − RAW) when Dr > RAW. Water above field capacity goes to a gravity store holding up to 10% of the root depth (in mm). It drains each day at most 60% of that store, at the slowest saturated conductivity in the metre below the roots × a drainage-class factor (well drained 1, moderately well 0.5, somewhat poorly 0.25, poorly 0.1, very poorly 0.05). Those three numbers (10%, 60%, the factors) are the model's own choices, not taken from a publication. Snow is stored on days at or below 0 °C and melts at 3.5 mm per °C-day, also unsourced. Surface flow passes to every lower neighbour in proportion to slope1.1, on a surface filled by Priority-Flood [22]. In tests, water in equals water out plus storage to within 0.1%.

4. Grass type, roots and season

How thirsty each grass is

Water use is the weather's reference figure (section 5) times a crop coefficient for the grass. The coefficients are the developer's own: a weighted average of values from a number of studies, weighted toward what applies to residential turf. The original citations have been lost, so the individual studies cannot be listed here. The table gives a range per species and per watering level, and the model takes the middle of each range. For Kentucky bluegrass that is 0.90 at normal watering, 0.725 at deficit and 0.50 at dormancy; tall fescue 0.90, 0.70 and 0.50. Published values for comparison: FAO-56 gives 0.90 to 0.95 for cool-season turf [1], and University of California work gives an annual average of 0.80 for cool-season and 0.60 for warm-season grass [5]. The deficit figure for bluegrass sits inside a range of 71 to 110% of reference use found acceptable in a national trial (NTEP 2018–20).

At deficit or dormancy the schedule waters for the lower coefficient while the grass keeps drawing at the normal one. The shortfall dries the soil past the stress line, and the model counts those days.

One coefficient applies all season. There is no month-by-month curve, and mowing height, fertilizer and thinning in shade are not modeled.

Roots

Each species has a working root depth, averaged over the species ticked: Kentucky bluegrass and perennial ryegrass 8 in, tall fescue and bermudagrass 12 in, with sources in the research notes behind the tool (K-State's MF-2803, for example, advises wetting bluegrass and tall fescue lawns 6 to 8 in deep [31]).

Roots are not the same depth all year. The model grows and loses roots day by day with the soil temperature 4 in down, following Beard and Daniel's finding that soil temperature about 6 in down tracked creeping bentgrass root growth more closely than the other conditions they measured [18]. The model uses a 5-day running mean of it. The depth never drops below 40% of the working depth.

Roots run on the soil's temperature, not the leaves'. Cool-season roots keep growing slowly whenever the soil is above freezing, even while the grass is dormant and barely making food. Kentucky bluegrass root cells keep dividing just above 0 °C (Bogart's work at Michigan State), and perennial ryegrass has been found making new roots in every month of the year. So the model starts bluegrass root growth at about 1 °C (34 °F) soil, reaching about 40% of its peak rate at 5 °C (41 °F), and ryegrass at 2 °C. Root loss slows in cold soil as well, so roots hold through the winter and the lawn starts spring with them. Warm-season grasses are different: their roots barely grow below about 10 °C (50 °F) soil, and after green-up in spring the old roots die back and new ones take about three weeks to start, which the model follows.

On the developer's lawn this gives bluegrass about 6.8 in of roots in May and June and about 5.3 in by September. Only the bentgrass curve is fitted to measured data. The others are estimates held to published temperature limits.

The water the roots can use is counted layer by layer, down to that day's depth. A dense or sandy layer within the root zone changes the count. Layers that physically stop roots are not yet modeled.

The growing season

Watering is planned only in the growing season. It starts on the first spring day the grass's growth potential reaches 20% (the curve is Gelernter and Stowell's [17]; the 20% threshold is the developer's rule) and ends when the 5-day mean soil temperature falls below 7 °C (cool-season) or 13 °C (warm-season).

5. Weather and water use

The weather's demand for water is the "reference evapotranspiration": what a well-watered, mown grass surface would use. It is computed each day by the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith equation [1] from NASA POWER's temperature, humidity, wind and sunlight. The code reproduces FAO-56's own worked example (3.88 against the published 3.9 mm a day).

Rain comes from PRISM, a gauge-based 4 km grid. PRISM replaced NASA POWER's rain after the developer noticed POWER's July rain ran high: ten-year July medians were 3.94 in from POWER against 2.94 in from PRISM.

The hours of a storm matter on slow soils. Rain falling faster than the ground takes it in runs off even when the day's total would soak in. The model shapes each rainy day by the hours seen at the nearest airport (or on radar, for recent weeks) and keeps PRISM's total.

Known open question On the developer's lawn, the model's June and July reference ET is 15 to 20% above the figures Michigan State University's Enviroweather network gives for that area (figures read off its site by the developer, not pulled from its data). The sunlight input was checked and is not the cause: NASA POWER's sunlight matched seven SURFRAD research radiometers to within 1% (ratio 0.991, 2018–2024). Running FAO-56 on nearby stations' own measured weather gives values at or above the model's, so the gap may come from a difference in method rather than in the weather data. It is not settled. If Enviroweather's lower figure is right, the model's summer amounts are high by roughly that much.

6. The shade map

The shade map is a companion tool on Lawn Mapper that maps sun and shade across a lawn through the year, from the same lidar. The water model does no shade calculation of its own. It runs the shade map's own code in the browser, so the two always agree, and takes these from it:

  • Light. Each 1 m square's daily light as a share of open sky, worked out for every third day of each month and for the summer months. The water account uses each month's share for that square.
  • Which trees keep their leaves. Evergreen or deciduous, for wind shelter (a bare crown lets more wind through) and for cooler air near trees.
  • Fences the user drew, with their height and material, for the fence warmth in section 7.
  • Trees the user erased because they are gone since the lidar flight. An erased tree casts no shade, gives no shelter and cools nothing.
  • The growing season and soil temperature of a typical year, which set when watering is planned (section 4) and drive the root model.
  • The ground. Both maps use one ground surface, cell for cell, so a square in one map is the same square in the other.

How the water model turns these into water use is section 7. The rest of this section, how the shade map works and how it has been checked, is written and kept current by the shade map's developers.

The shade map's own description is being written by its developers and will appear here.

7. How sun, shade, heat and wind change water use

Shade

Each square's daily light, as a share of open sky, comes from the shade map (section 6). Grass in shade uses less water, but not in proportion to the light. Part of its use is driven by the air (warmth, dryness and wind), and that part continues in shade. Danielson and Feldhake's lysimeter work on Kentucky bluegrass [6] showed this, and the model computes the air-driven share for each day from the Penman–Monteith equation. On the developer's lawn in summer it is about a quarter of open-lawn use.

Check against published measurements: the shadiest tenth of the developer's lawn is modeled at 0.39 of open-lawn use, inside the range measured elsewhere: water use two to three times lower in shade than in sun in a study of lawns in several U.S. cities [7], and 61% of open-lawn use under full tree cover in Los Angeles [8]. The shadiest 2% comes out at 0.22, below every published measurement, so the model may under-water the very darkest spots.

Technical note Each day, grass use per square = ET0 × Kc × heat × [(1 − s)·L + s·A]. L is the square's light share for the month (or the net-radiation share below), s the day's aerodynamic share of the FAO-56 Penman–Monteith ET0 (the air-driven term over the whole; 0.3 when a day's weather is missing, after Danielson and Feldhake's roughly 30%), A the cut for cooler air near trees, and "heat" the paving and fence terms. Danielson and Feldhake's floor refitted from their appendix data was 2.08–2.37 mm/day (a share of 0.36–0.41) against the equation's 2.39 mm/day (0.43) on the same weeks' weather. No lower crop coefficient is used for shade-thinned turf: grass grown in shade used what sun grass did while it covered the ground (Feldhake, Butler and Danielson, 1985).

Light under leaves, and the night sky

Leaves let through more near-infrared light than visible light, so shade under trees is warmer than its brightness suggests. The model splits sunlight into its visible and near-infrared parts [19] and also counts how much open sky each square sees at night, since a square that sees open sky loses heat faster. Together these add 1 to 3% to use in shaded spots. The sun falls on each square's own slope: a slope facing the sun gets more of it than flat open ground, and one facing away gets less.

Technical note Each day's sunlight is split into direct and diffuse light (Spitters and others [19]), and each into visible and near-infrared halves; skylight is taken as about 1.4 times richer in visible light under a clear sky. A crown passing a share T of the visible light passes T0.8 of the near-infrared (an exponent of 0.7 to 1.0 is supported by published canopy measurements). Absorbed shortwave = (1 − 0.06)·visible + (1 − 0.40)·near-infrared, the turf reflectances in each band; open lawn comes back to FAO-56's 0.23 albedo. Net longwave is FAO-56's open-sky figure times the square's sky view, the view taken through crowns at their visible transparency, with leaves and walls at air temperature. FAO-56's figure falls as the air's vapour pressure rises and as the day's sunshine falls short of a clear day's, so damp and cloudy nights take less heat [1]; cloud at night is read from that day's sunshine, not measured. The direct beam is the shade map's share of open, level ground's, the sun taken onto the square's own slope (more than 1 on a slope facing the sun, held to 1.6; until 7 November 2026 it was held to 1 here).

Cooler air in shade

On sunny days, air near tree cover is cooler. A study in Madison, Wisconsin measured up to 1.3 °C cooler at full tree cover within 30 m [9]. The model cuts the air-driven part of use to match: 2 to 3% less in sun near trees, up to 8% less in deep shade. Since 7 November 2026 the shade of buildings counts as well as trees. That is the developer's judgement: the study measured tree cover only, and how much a house's shade cools the air around it has not been measured here.

Technical note On growing-season days the day's maximum temperature is lowered by 1.3 °C × the share of ground under tree crowns within 30 m × the day's sunniness (its clearness over 0.75, at most 1), the minimum by 0.3 of that, vapour held, and the aerodynamic term recomputed. Ziter and others found the cooling grows faster than linearly above about 40% cover, most strongly when cover is counted over 60 to 90 m; the model keeps it linear, over 30 m. The share counted is of ground under tree crowns, buildings and low structures (sheds, walls) together, held to 1.

Wind

A yard's wind is reduced by the buildings and trees around it. The yard average follows Heisler's measurements at houses in Pennsylvania [10]; the developer's lawn comes out at 42% of open wind. Each square then gets its own shelter by direction from fences, hedges, trees and houses, using the USDA wind erosion model's barrier curves [12] and published rules for the calm zone behind buildings, weighted by the nearest airport's wind directions. These curves come from field windbreaks and wind tunnels, not yards.

Technical note Yard wind share = 1 − x / (24 + 1.1x), with x the percent of ground within 90 m under buildings and crowns (Heisler's fit [10]); FAO-56 Penman–Monteith is recomputed each day at the reduced wind. Per square: 16 directions × 3 rays out to 150 m over the lidar and building outlines. Fences, hedges and crowns use the WEPS barrier curve by porosity [12] (written for the wind's drag at the ground, its friction velocity, and taken here as the share of wind; valid to a porosity of 0.8) (crowns pass 0.2 of the wind in leaf, 0.5 bare, evergreens 0.2; a crown lifted on a trunk gives no shelter for 3 tree heights); houses use Röckle cavity and wake zones (cavity 0.1, wake 0.3 of the wind). The strongest shelter in each direction is taken, directions are weighted by the nearest airport's monthly daytime wind rose (speed-weighted, 10 years), and one factor per month holds the lawn's mean to the yard figure. Temperature and humidity are held when the wind drops, though sheltered air is warmer and more humid, so the cut may be a little large.

Fences and paving

A sunny fence board is warmer than the air and blocks part of the cold night sky. On a test fence the grass on the sunny side used 1.9% more, the shaded side about the same, and nothing changed beyond three fence heights. Grass within 7 m of paving, a brick or paver patio, or a roof uses up to 8% more at the edge, fading to nothing at 7 m. That figure is an estimate drawn from a desert field edge [11], in line with the 1.1 to 1.4 microclimate factors that California's landscape water guide gives as expert judgement [29]. No measurement of grass beside paving by direction was found.

Technical note Each face of a fence board is solved for its own temperature from the sun on it (through the trees), its convection (5.7 + 3.8u W/m²/K, a flat plate, at 1.5 m/s), its linearised radiation (emissivity 0.9) and the conductance through the board (wood about 6.3 W/m²/K, hollow vinyl 5): the face toward the grass is x1 = (S1(h + K) + S2K) / (h(h + 2K)) above air temperature. The grass gains that face's extra longwave over the part of its sky the fence fills, and loses the cold sky it would have seen. The fence's shade and reflected sun are the shade map's. Paving, pavers and walls: +8% at the edge, falling linearly to 0 at 7 m, half as much when the hot surface lies north or east of the grass; asphalt, concrete and pavers are not told apart (pavers counted since 7 November 2026).

The heat map and surface temperature

The map's Heat view shows, square by square, what warms or cools the lawn for a typical day of any month from April to October. It adds nothing to the water account; it shows the same terms the account already uses.

  • Energy. The heat each square gains or loses against open, level lawn in full sun. It is split into four parts: the sun through what stands over it and on its own slope, the heat kept from the sky by trees and buildings overhead, fences, and the heat off paving and walls nearby.
  • Night sky. The heat each square loses to the sky. Open ground loses the most.
  • Air. How much cooler the air is in shade on a sunny afternoon.
  • Surface temperature. An estimate of the grass surface's temperature at the peak of a sunny afternoon and before dawn. It adds each square's own wind and how wet its ground is, kept watered or with rain alone. Watered grass spends most of the sun evaporating water and stays within a few degrees of the air. Dry grass cannot, and heats. Wind carries the heat away, so a sheltered corner runs hotter by day and colder before dawn.

On the developer's lawn in July, kept watered, the afternoon surface runs at 81 to 83 °F against 81 °F air. With rain alone it runs at 81 to 102 °F, the open sunny lawn the hottest. Before dawn the surface runs 1 to 3 °F under the air, the open lawn coldest.

The surface temperature has not been checked against a thermometer. The soil's own temperature, spot by spot, is not modelled: roots use the area's measured soil temperature.

Technical note Energy terms are 24-hour means in W/m², against open level lawn: absorbed shortwave as above; net longwave times (1 − sky view); the fence terms; paving as its extra evaporation (edge factor − 1) × 2.45 MJ/kg × Kc × ET0. Surface temperature: Ts − Ta = (Rn − G − LE) / (ρcp/ra + 4εσTa3).
  • Afternoon: Rn is the square's absorbed sun at the day's peak (the day's total as a half-sine over FAO-56's day length), less its longwave loss, plus fences and paving scaled the same way. G = 0.1Rn (FAO-56, hourly by day). LE = Ks × 1.26 × Δ/(Δ + γ) × (Rn − G) (Priestley and Taylor [34]), with Ks FAO-56's water-stress coefficient averaged over the month's days of the ten-year run [1]. The air is the month's mean high, less the shade cooling above.
  • Before dawn: Rn is the longwave loss alone, G = 0.5Rn (FAO-56, hourly by night), LE = 0, and the air is the month's mean low.
  • ra = 208/u2 s/m (FAO-56's clipped grass), with u2 the month's mean wind times the square's own share of it, at least 0.5 m/s; ε = 0.98.
  • Not included: hour-by-hour shade (each square's share of the day's sun is used at the peak), heat stored in paving and given back at night, dew, and the wind's drop at night.

8. Slope, runoff, roofs and paving

The ground surface comes from the lidar at 1 m. Runoff follows the USDA curve number method (TR-55 [2]) by the soil's hydrologic group, steepened for slope by the method in the SWAT model's documentation [13]. On days with hourly rain, any rain falling faster than the ground's intake also runs off. Runoff flows on to lower squares, so a square below a slope or a downspout gets more water than falls on it.

The ground's intake rate comes from the table of maximum sprinkler rates by soil texture and slope in Hunter's scheduling notes [28], capped at the soil survey's measured permeability of the top 6 in. On the developer's lawn this gives 0.14 in an hour, which matched the developer's experience of a slow lawn after an earlier version read it far too fast.

Technical note Curve numbers: open space in good condition, groups A/B/C/D 39/61/74/80; other ground in fair condition 49/69/79/84; paving 98; pavers as gravel 76/85/89/91 [2]. Initial abstraction Ia = 0.2S. Hawkins and others [14] argue for 0.05S, which gives more runoff from small rains; that change has not been made. The curve number method was built for storm events and is applied here to daily totals.

Roofs are found from the lidar, with the photo used to drop green tree tops. The rule that tells a building from a tree is the shade map's, the same one every tool on the site uses; it also counts low structures such as sheds and walls as roof. Against a 1 m land cover map of 23 residential squares in Virginia, Maryland and Pennsylvania, the rule found 83% of roof squares exactly and 95% within 1 m (measured before sheds and walls were added). The user marks where each downspout empties. Each rain day the first 0.5 mm wets the roof, a reading of the Texas rainwater harvesting manual's rule for setting aside the first, dirtiest water (at least 10 gallons per 1,000 sq ft, about 0.4 mm) [27], and the rest goes to the downspout's spot on the lawn.

Paving is found from the lidar and the photo, using the user's own traced lawn as the reference for "green". On 11 test squares it was right on 85.5% of squares and found 85% of the paving. Every test so far is in the mid-Atlantic.

Low spots are the hollows where water would collect if it did not soak in. On made-up ground with known hollows, a hollow 8 m or more across and 5 cm or more deep is found most of the time. One 5 m across is found about half the time unless it is deeper than 7 cm, and one 3 m across is nearly invisible. This is a limit of the lidar's point spacing and noise.

9. Water from below

The water table

Where the water table is close to the surface, it lifts water up into the roots and can keep ground wet whatever the weather. The model starts from the water on the national maps within about 1 km (streams, ponds, lakes and wetlands), where the water table meets the surface. Between them the water table is taken to rise about half as fast as the ground. That rate was fitted to 227 shallow wells in two Michigan counties, where the height above nearby water ranked the depth to water with a correlation of 0.56 to 0.59 and a typical error of 1.7 m. The water table is taken to be highest in April and 0.75 m (about 2.5 ft) lower by October, except near year-round water. Where the soil survey lists a shallower wet layer, the survey wins.

Water rising into the roots follows AquaCrop's equations for four soil classes [4], limited to what refills the roots and shutting off as they wet. On the developer's lawn the water table is 12 ft or more down and lifts almost nothing. On a customer's lawn that stayed wet weeks after rain in a dry October, the model now puts the water table about 6 in down from November to June, which fits what was seen.

Water held up by a slow layer

A slow layer partway down, such as dense clay under sand, can hold water up above the true water table. That water stays wet on flat ground and runs downhill along the top of the layer elsewhere, coming out lower down as a seep. The model finds such layers in the soil survey: a layer passing a tenth or less of what the soil above it passes, and no more than about 3.4 in a day, or a cemented layer or hardpan the survey names. Water draining from the roots builds up on it, leaks through it at the layer's own rate [16], and moves downhill along it to lower squares, the approach of Cornell's Soil Moisture Routing model for soils over hardpans [15]. The layer is set deeper under rises and shallower under hollows, by the survey's range of depths.

The layer's speed is the least known number here. Where the survey records water standing on a layer in certain months, the model slows the layer until it would hold water at that depth under those months' rain. This has been run on two lawns but not checked against a probe.

Topsoil on sand

A finer soil laid over sand holds water above the boundary, because the sand takes none until the topsoil is nearly soaked (a capillary barrier) [30]. When a user says loamy topsoil was brought in over a sandy soil, the model adds about 2.2 mm of holding per cm of topsoil (about 0.9 in for 4 in) and lifts no water across the boundary. This rests on standard soil water curves and has not been measured on a lawn.

10. Zones and the schedule

First the model waters every square on its own, ideally, over the ten years. In the lawn's thirstiest month it records how often each square would need water, then groups squares by that interval using fixed breaks at 2, 3, 4.5, 7 and 10 days. Small groups join their neighbours, groups with similar rhythms merge, and the result is at most four zones. The breaks were chosen so that the same lawn always gives the same zones. A user can draw their own zones instead, and mapped sprinklers replace the zones with their valves.

Then each zone is watered as one: on the morning its average square would cross the stress line, refilled to full plus that day's use. Each watering is capped at what the ground can take in two soak cycles a day (the developer's rule), each taken as one hour at the ground's intake rate (an assumption a soak test would check). The amount is then raised by a scheduling multiplier, because sprinklers never water evenly. Using the Irrigation Association's formula, SM = 1 / (0.4 + 0.6 × DU), with a distribution uniformity of 0.65 for a typical home spray system, the multiplier is 1.27. Once sprinklers are mapped, each zone's own modeled uniformity is used instead.

Refilling to full plus the day's use means some water drains past the roots on fast soils: about 10 to 20% beyond the grass's use in a one-square test on loam or sand. Lighter, more frequent watering was tried and dropped, because it kept the soil full so that rain ran off.

12. Assumptions and weak points

In rough order of how much they could change a watering plan:

  1. The weather's water demand. The 15–20% gap with Enviroweather (section 5). If Enviroweather is right, summer amounts are high by about that much.
  2. The soil survey describes native soil, and a lawn is usually built ground. Graded fill and compacted topsoil can take in water far more slowly than the survey says. Measured urban lawns ran 0.1 to 0.35 times the survey's rate when new or compacted, and about the same as the survey when old [20, 21]. The model makes no adjustment for compaction. The developer's own lot is thought to be heavier clay than the survey's loam; no soil pit has settled it.
  3. Crop coefficients from the developer's weighted average of several studies whose citations have been lost, one value all season.
  4. The model's own numbers: the drainage-class factors, the 60% a day drainage, the 10% of root depth held above field capacity, the one-hour soak cycle, and the snowmelt rate. Each is a reasonable choice, and none comes from a measurement.
  5. Runoff from small rains depends on the curve number method's initial abstraction, kept at the standard 0.2S.
  6. One weather cell per lot. Rain is from a 4 km grid and the rest from about 50 km. Microclimate comes only from shade, wind, fences, paving and nearby trees.
  7. The lidar is as old as its flight. Regrading, new patios and removed trees since then are invisible unless the user marks them.
  8. Things not modeled: trees drawing water from the lawn through their roots, dew, sump pumps and foundation drains, water from a neighbour's yard moving underground, and soil layers that stop roots.

A programming error stopped it reading the shade map, and the tool carried on without saying so. Watering figures from those days are too high in shaded parts of a lawn. 13. How it will be checked next

Soil moisture readings. Since November 2026, readings are taken in the field with a hand meter at marked spots, a few days apart, and kept with each spot's position. The meter in use reads a relative scale, not calibrated water content, so the first test is one of order. Do the spots the model calls driest read driest, and does the order hold from visit to visit? That tests the zone pattern directly. Matching amounts would take oven-dried soil cores.

A soak test (a can and a ruler, or a ring infiltrometer) would test the intake rate and the one-hour soak cycle, which set the cap on each watering.

Wet spots after rain, photographed and compared with the low-spot map. The target set before any field data: at least 7 of 10 real wet spots found within 5 m, and at least 7 of 10 reported spots really wet.

Catch cups and gauge readings at sprinkler heads, for the evenness and pressure models.

Results will be added to this paper as they come in, including results that go against the model.

14. How it compares with other tools

Each part of this model is well studied. A search of the research literature and commercial tools in November 2026 found no other tool that combines them for a single residential lawn at 1 m. The nearest relatives:

  • Smart irrigation controllers (the EPA WaterSense category) use the same kind of inputs (soil type, slope, sun or shade, root depth, plant type), entered as one setting per zone and adjusted daily for weather.
  • Precision golf course irrigation maps fairways on a 2.5 m grid and draws management zones from measured soil moisture and terrain [24]. It measures in the field rather than modeling, and it found that results on one fairway do not always carry to the next.
  • Watershed models such as DHSVM [25] and the Soil Moisture Routing model [15] use much the same physics cell by cell (sunlight by slope and aspect under a canopy, layered soil, water moving sideways underground) at 10 to 100 m, checked against stream gauges.
  • Urban climate models such as UT&C [26] and ENVI-met couple shade, heat, soil water and irrigation for a street or block, to study cooling rather than watering schedules.
